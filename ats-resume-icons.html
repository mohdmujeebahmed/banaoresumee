The Issue

What's Actually Going On: ATS Resume Icons

ATS resume icons refers to the practice of using small graphic symbols — a phone icon, an envelope icon, a location pin, a checkmark bullet — in place of plain text words or characters, and the specific parsing risk this substitution introduces.

Icons have become a common feature of modern resume templates, used to replace the words "Phone," "Email," and "Location" with recognizable small graphics, or to replace standard bullet points with checkmarks, arrows, or other decorative symbols. Visually, this can look clean and contemporary. Structurally, from a parsing standpoint, it introduces a specific and fairly severe risk.

The core issue is that most icons are inserted either as small embedded images or as special font-based glyph characters, neither of which reliably map back to readable, extractable text the way a standard word or character does. When a parser processes the document, an icon frequently extracts as nothing at all — a blank space where a phone number label, or a bullet point, should logically be.

This is a narrower version of the broader formatting-mistakes problem, worth its own dedicated attention because icons are extremely common in contemporary resume templates and the failure mode is specific enough to deserve a clear, standalone explanation.

Root cause

Why This Happens

Icons are typically inserted into a resume in one of two ways, and both carry parsing risk. The first is as a small embedded image file — a genuine picture of a phone, dropped into the document. Parsers that extract text content have no mechanism for reading meaning out of an embedded image, so this method essentially guarantees the icon extracts as nothing.

The second, more common method in modern templates, is using special icon font characters — where a particular font maps certain keystrokes to icon glyphs instead of standard letters. This can sometimes extract as a strange, meaningless character or symbol rather than blank space, which is arguably an even worse outcome, since it can introduce visible garbage characters into what should be a clean, professional-looking extracted resume.

Icons are appealing to template designers and job seekers alike because they create a scannable, modern visual style efficiently — a small graphic communicates "phone number" faster to a human eye than the word "Phone:" does. This genuine visual efficiency is exactly what doesn't translate to a parsing engine, which has no visual scanning capability at all and relies entirely on the underlying text content.

There's also a risk-awareness gap specific to this issue. Because icons are so visually appealing and widely used in popular templates, many job seekers reasonably assume that if a feature is common and popular, it must be broadly safe — without realizing that popularity in template design and safety in ATS parsing are two completely unrelated qualities.

Mechanics

How the ATS Handles This

When a parser encounters an embedded image icon, it typically has no text-extraction pathway for it at all — the parser is looking for text content, and an image, regardless of what it visually depicts, simply isn't text content. The result is usually a gap in the extracted output exactly where the icon sat, with no equivalent word substituted in its place.

When a parser encounters an icon rendered through a special font glyph, the outcome is more variable and depends on the specific font and encoding involved. Some systems may extract nothing, effectively the same outcome as an embedded image. Others may extract a placeholder character, a strange symbol, or in rare cases even a completely unrelated letter or number, depending on how that particular glyph happens to be encoded in the underlying font's character map.

Either way, the practical consequence is the same: information that was clearly communicated visually — this is a phone number, this is an email address — is lost or garbled in the version of your resume the ATS actually stores and searches against, even though nothing about the underlying information itself was ever unclear or incomplete.

Pitfalls

Common Mistakes

Mistake

Using icons in place of contact info labels

A phone or envelope icon instead of the word "Phone:" or "Email:" is one of the most damaging uses, since it directly risks losing key contact information.

Mistake

Using icon-based bullet points

Checkmarks, arrows, or other decorative bullet icons frequently extract as nothing, sometimes removing the visual separation between distinct points entirely.

Mistake

Assuming a popular template's icon usage means it's safe

Widespread use in modern templates reflects visual design trends, not verified ATS safety.

Mistake

Using icons for section headers

A graphic icon next to or replacing the word "Experience" risks that section header not being recognized by the parser at all.

Mistake

Not testing a template before relying on it

The only reliable way to know if a specific template's icons cause a problem is to actually run a scan or parse test on the exported file.

Mistake

Using social media or website icons without an accompanying text link

An icon-only link to LinkedIn or a portfolio can be entirely lost without a corresponding plain-text URL nearby.

Walkthrough

Step-by-Step Fixes

01

Identify every icon currently used in your resume

Contact info symbols, bullet point icons, section header graphics — list each specific instance.

02

Replace contact info icons with plain text labels

"Phone:", "Email:", "Location:" as actual words, followed by the corresponding information.

03

Replace icon-based bullets with a standard bullet character or hyphen

A simple round bullet or dash communicates the same visual structure without any parsing risk.

04

Replace any icon-based section headers with bold plain text

"Experience" in bold, standard text — no accompanying graphic needed for the label to be recognized.

05

Add plain-text URLs alongside any social or web icons

Even if you keep a small icon for visual style, include the actual link as visible text nearby as a backup.

06

Test the rebuilt resume with a scanner or parser tool

Confirm every previously icon-represented piece of information now extracts correctly as readable text.

07

Do a final visual review after the text-based rebuild

Confirm the resume still looks clean and professional using plain text and standard formatting instead of icons.

Get it right

Best Practices

Do

Use plain text labels for all contact information

"Phone:", "Email:", "LinkedIn:" as actual words are the single safest choice for this common use case.

Do

Use standard bullet characters instead of icon bullets

A simple round or square bullet point communicates the same list structure without any parsing risk.

Do

Keep section headers as bold plain text

No graphic element needed — bold, clear text is both fully parser-safe and perfectly readable to a human eye.

Do

Include plain-text URLs, not just icon links

Ensures the actual link information is captured as extractable text even if a small icon is kept for visual style.

Do

Test any template with icons before fully committing to it

A quick scan or parse test will tell you definitively whether a specific template's icon usage causes extraction problems.

Do

Prioritize clarity over visual novelty for anything essential

Contact information and section headers are too important to risk on a stylistic choice that may not extract correctly.

Real world

Real Examples

Fresher using a popular icon-heavy template

Contact information was represented entirely by phone, email, and location icons with no accompanying text labels. A parse test showed all three icons extracted as blank space, meaning the resume had no recoverable contact information at all in the extracted version.

Marketing candidate using checkmark bullet icons

Used a stylish checkmark icon instead of a standard bullet point for every line under her experience section. Testing showed the checkmarks extracted as stray, meaningless characters scattered throughout the text, creating a garbled impression despite clean original formatting.

Consultant with an icon-only LinkedIn link

Included a small LinkedIn icon linking to his profile, with no visible text URL anywhere on the resume. The icon extracted as nothing, meaning the ATS-stored version of his resume had no trace of his LinkedIn profile at all.

Job seeker who rebuilt an icon-based header

Used a small folder icon next to the word "Experience" as a section header. After a parse test showed the section wasn't being recognized correctly, removing the icon and keeping just the bold text "Experience" resolved the section-detection issue immediately.
